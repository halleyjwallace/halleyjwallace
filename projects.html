For my Robot Perception midterm at Cornell, I modeled a virtual RGB pinhole camera mounted on the end effector of a seven-joint KUKA iiwa arm in PyBullet, loading the robot from its URDF. I then tested how image measurements depend on camera pose, field of view, target size, and occlusion.

Each test compared two independent estimates of where a red cube appears in the image: a mathematical prediction that projects the cube's known 3D position through the camera model, and an OpenCV measurement taken directly from the rendered pixels. The two agreed to within 2.91 pixels in the baseline case on a 640 × 480 image. This validates the geometric model in simulation; it is not a physical-camera calibration, and it does not model image noise or lens distortion.

Mathematical Modeling

World to camera frame
qC = RWCT (xT − xC)
Pinhole projection to pixels
û = cx − f qx / qz,   v̂ = cy − f qy / qz
Field of view to focal length
f = H / (2 tan(θ / 2))
Sensitivity to lateral motion
∂û / ∂qx = −f / qz
Sensitivity to camera tilt
dû / dα = f [1 + (qx / qz)2]
Apparent target size
wpx ≈ f L / qz
Pixel error
epx = √[(uobs − û)2 + (vobs − v̂)2]

Technical Methods

  • Robot setup: loaded the fixed-base arm from its KUKA URDF and solved inverse kinematics for the end-effector pose (0.00886 mm position error)
  • Coordinate transforms: carried the camera mounting offset and tilt through rotation matrices from the end-effector frame to the camera frame
  • Image measurement: HSV color thresholding, largest-contour selection, and bounding-box centers in OpenCV
  • Controlled experiments: varied one parameter at a time across camera translation, field of view, tilt, cube size, and line of sight
  • Error analysis: compared predicted and measured pixel locations, displacements, and size ratios

Key Results

  • Baseline: predicted (380, 280) px vs. measured (382.5, 281.5) px, a 2.91 px error
  • Translation: moving the camera about 0.10 m shifted the cube 48 px in both model and measurement, matching the predicted 480 px/m sensitivity at 0.50 m depth
  • Tilt (0° to 20°): 102.11 px predicted vs. 104.5 px measured horizontal shift
  • Field of view (50° to 110°): detected cube width fell from 108 px to 36 px
  • Target size (4 to 10 cm): image width ratio of 2.55 vs. a physical size ratio of 2.50
  • Occlusion: a blocked cube went undetected even though its center still projected inside the field of view, showing that being in the field of view does not guarantee visibility

For this course project, we are developing a forearm-worn skin-stretch device to help surgeons and surgical trainees distinguish soft tissue from stiff structures, such as gallstones, during minimally invasive gallbladder surgery. Force sensors on the jaws of laparoscopic forceps will measure grasping force, and a tactor on the forearm will stretch the skin in proportion to that force, keeping the hand free to operate.

In laparoscopic surgery, trocar friction, abdominal wall elasticity, and the fulcrum effect all distort the forces a surgeon feels. Earlier forearm skin-stretch work raised stiffness identification from 50% to 73% and cut peak force by 36%, but with a palpation probe (Saudrais et al., 2025). Our project extends the approach to grasping, using the forceps surgeons rely on to hold and retract the gallbladder.

Concept sketch: force-sensitive resistors on the grasper jaws signal an Arduino, which drives a servo and rack-and-pinion to displace a forearm tactor.

Planned Validation

Five users will rank three objects of different stiffness that they cannot see, using a standard laparoscopic grasper and our haptic grasper. We will compare how accurately they identify stiffness with each one.

Project Plan

  • Finalize components, order materials, and complete the initial CAD
  • Assemble the rack-and-pinion mechanism and validate the force sensors
  • Find the force-displacement relationship and integrate the system
  • Run the user study and collect data
  • Finalize the setup for the demo

Skills I Will Build

  • Haptic device design: a wearable, servo-driven rack-and-pinion skin-stretch mechanism
  • Sensor integration: instrumenting forceps jaws with force-sensitive resistors and validating their signals
  • Embedded control: mapping force readings to servo motion on an Arduino in real time
  • System characterization: relating grasping force, object stiffness, and tactor displacement
  • Human-centered evaluation: designing a user study around perception of stiffness

Led mechanical design and validation of a frugal, reusable in-line suction filter for operating rooms in resource-limited clinical settings, improving suction safety and reliability by 50%. Single-use filters are routinely reused in low-resource hospitals due to cost — our device provides a safe, sterilizable alternative where no cost-effective reusable solution previously existed.

We conducted material and mesh analysis, porosimeter testing, and Bacterial Filtration Efficiency (BFE) validation confirming performance across 30 sterilization cycles. I performed structural and fluid-structure simulations and maintained ISO 13485 design control documentation throughout.

Designed and prototyped a wearable ankle band for early detection of preeclampsia — a leading cause of maternal mortality, particularly affecting Black women in Georgia. The device uses a PPG sensor for continuous blood pressure monitoring and a flex sensor to quantify ankle edema, two key early indicators of preeclampsia.

The physical prototype integrates an Arduino Nano with sensors mounted on a cloth band for comfortable at-home use. A parallel computational prototype used Python image analysis of colorimetric urinalysis assays (Congo Red Dye and Tetrabromophenol Blue) to detect proteinuria — another preeclampsia biomarker. The project emphasized high-specificity design so any positive result would be confirmed by a medical professional.

Key Outcomes

  • Functional PPG-based blood pressure monitoring and flex-sensor edema detection
  • Parallel computational prototype: Python image analysis of urinalysis assays
  • High-specificity design for at-home use
  • Addressed maternal health disparities in under-resourced Georgia communities

Designed and built a wearable lumbar sensor band to monitor lower back posture and movement. The device integrates embedded electronics into a flexible, form-fitting band worn at the lumbar region, enabling non-invasive tracking of spinal position and muscle activity throughout daily activity.

The CAD model was developed in Fusion 360, and the physical prototype was assembled and tested on a mannequin and team members. The project aimed to provide a low-cost, wearable solution for monitoring lumbar health outside of a clinical setting.
